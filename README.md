# Digital Diorama

**The classroom shoebox diorama, reimagined as a living 3D habitat you can build, explore, code and present.**

Students pick a biome, shape the land with landforms, sculpting and painting, fill it with real species from a library of nearly 350 procedural plants, animals, fungi and habitat features, label what they learned on toothpick flags, then bring it to life with Scratch-style block code — and finally present it with a narrated guided tour. Along the way they earn XP and badges, fill a field journal with the species they discover, and complete habitat quests for up to three stars. Runs in any modern desktop or mobile browser, and feels like an app on phones (top bar, bottom tab bar, pull-out trays; it can be added to the home screen to run full screen). Projects and progress save on the device, with no accounts.

Most 3D models come from the procedural three.js assets in [`References/`](References), imported in place and code-split so each one only loads when used. Trees, wildflowers, grasses and a few marine animals, fish and fungi come from the app's own procedural generators in `src/assets/flora` and `src/assets/builtin`. The animal modules carry this app's revisions, each marked in the code (*Digital Diorama extension* / *Digital Diorama revision*): a `specOverride` hook that lets related species such as a Bactrian camel or a blue jay share one rig, painted coat textures, smooth shading and glossy eyes in the mammal and bird rigs, mammal hind legs rebuilt from real skeletal proportions with hooves, paws and planted-foot gaits, and reshaped bodies, humps, heads and limb roots (see [Mammal legs and gaits](#mammal-legs-and-gaits)), an African elephant with its own builder and clips, every other mammal laid out the same way from a measured reference animal (see [Measured layouts](#mammal-legs-and-gaits)), birds that walk on planted feet and perch upright, faces and bodies reshaped species by species (jaws that fit the lips, real foreheads and cheeks; rebuilt seal, dolphin and whales), and species-by-species accuracy fixes made in the [animal workbench](#animal-workbench).

## Quick start

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npm run dev
```

Open the printed address. The dev server listens on your network too, so a phone or tablet on the same Wi-Fi can open the *Network* address. For a production build:

```bash
npm run build
```

```bash
npm run preview
```

The build in `dist/` is a static site (relative paths), so it can be hosted on GitHub Pages, Netlify, a school server, or opened from any static file host.

Deep links: `#new=savanna` opens a new (starter) diorama of a biome (`forest`, `taiga`, `grassland`, `savanna`, `rainforest`, `desert`, `tundra`, `wetland`, `mountain`, `reef`, `kelp`, `shore`, `garden`, `blank`); add `&size=small|medium|large|huge` to pick its size, or `&model=1` for the finished example instead; `#open=<project id>` reopens a saved one. A diorama opened from the home screen shows its `#open=` address, so reloading the page reopens it.

### Publishing on GitHub Pages

Every push to `main` builds the app and publishes it with GitHub Pages ([`.github/workflows/pages.yml`](.github/workflows/pages.yml)). Pull requests run the same build as a check, without publishing.

- **Turn it on once:** Settings → Pages → Build and deployment → Source: **GitHub Actions**. The site then lives at `https://<owner>.github.io/<repo>/`, and Actions → *Deploy to GitHub Pages* → **Run workflow** republishes it at any time.
- **Private repositories:** Pages for a private repository needs a paid GitHub plan (Pro, Team or Enterprise), and the published site is public on the internet either way.
- **Saved projects stay with the address:** dioramas are stored in each browser for the site they were made on, so ones saved on `localhost` won't appear on the Pages site. Move them with *Download project file* and *Open a file*.

## What students can do

### 🏠 Home
- **One screen, like a phone app:** a top bar (with your level and Settings), the home itself in the middle and, on phones, a bottom tab bar: **Home · Expeditions · My dioramas · Journal**. The home picks up where you left off with a big *Continue* card, and shows your ranger profile and your **Ranger training** progress (the next lesson, or the one to carry on with). First-timers get a short introduction, *How it works* in three steps (pick a habitat → bring it to life → earn ⭐⭐ to unlock more) and a *Start your first expedition* button instead.
- **Trays instead of long lists:** Expeditions, My dioramas and the Journal pull out from behind the tab bar over the home, to browse and load from. **Expeditions** is the *ranger path*: habitats grouped by the step that opens them, as small postcards (with *New*, *Done*, stars and how many of its species you've found); locked ones say how many more expeditions they need. Tap an open one for its briefing, name the diorama, choose the **size of the box** (Small, Medium, Large, Huge — or any size once unlocked) and **Start expedition!** **My dioramas** shows your saved dioramas newest first: tap to open, or use ⋯ to download or delete one; *Open file* loads a `.diorama.json`, and a search box appears once there are more than six. Tap the open tab again, tap the dimmed home, press Escape or drag the tray down to put it away.
- On wide screens (and phones held sideways) the tabs sit in the top bar and the trays are wider, with bigger postcards.

### 🎓 Ranger training: learning by doing
New users learn the studio inside their own first diorama, one small step at a time, by doing each thing themselves rather than reading about it.
- **A welcome, not a wall of text.** The first time a diorama opens, a welcome offers **Show me around** (about 8 minutes) or *I'll explore on my own*. Either way every lesson stays under **🎓 Learn** in the top bar (or `H`).
- **20 short lessons in 6 units**, ordered like the ranger path:
  1. 🌱 *Your first habitat*: Meet your studio · Look around · Bring it to life · Move, turn & resize · Discover species
  2. 💚 *Keep it healthy*: Ecosystem health · Who eats whom? · Quests & stars
  3. 🏔️ *Shape the land*: Landforms · Paint & water
  4. 📝 *Tell its story*: Labels & notes · Day & night · Walk inside
  5. 🧩 *Bring it to life*: Your first script · Weather · Sculpt by hand
  6. 🔭 *Present and go further*: Give a guided tour · Seasons · The Area tool · Roam the wild

  Lessons about tools further along the path unlock with those tools (Code and Weather at *Trail Scout*, tours at *Explorer*…).
- **Each step points at the real control and waits for you to use it.** A lesson card says what to do; a glowing ring (dimming the rest of the screen) marks the button, panel or tab; a hand over the 3D view acts out gestures such as drag, pinch, two-finger slide, tap or press-and-hold, hovering over a real plant or animal when you need to tap one. The step moves on when the diorama shows it was done: the camera turned or zoomed, a producer or a plant-eater was added, something was moved, turned or resized, a hill was stamped, the ground painted, a label written, it became night, you walked in, a script ran… Read-only steps have *Next*.
- **No dead ends.** If the control isn't on screen, the card shows the way there instead ("👉 First, switch to 🔨 Build" with a *Go* button, "Tap 🛠️ Edit to see its tools", "Pick 🌿 Plants", "Open Land tools"), and controls scrolled out of their panel are scrolled into view. A step that was already done when it began shows ✓ with *Next*. A lesson that needs something first (living things, land rather than a habitat tile) says so and offers the lesson that helps.
- **Rewards and choice.** A finished lesson recaps what was learned, gives XP (once) and offers the next one. ✕ puts a lesson aside: the Learn button, the hub and the home screen offer to *carry on* from the same step, and a lesson in progress survives a reload.
- **Help at the moment it's needed:** quests have a **Show me how** button that starts their lesson, a completed expedition's *unlocked* card offers **Learn how** for every new tool, and the first visit to an empty box, to Build and to Code each shows a one-time tip pointing at the right place.
- **Learn & help** (the 🎓 Learn button, *More → Learn & help*, or the training card on the home screen) has the lessons by unit with progress, a searchable **How do I…?** (each answer can start its lesson), **Shortcuts** for the keyboard and touch gestures, and **Teachers**: a suggested first session, the ranger path, quests as a rubric, saving and sharing work, coding challenges, and the teacher-mode and tips switches.
- *Settings → Tips and the guided start* turns the welcome and the one-time tips off (or back on) for the device; lessons are always available.

### 🧭 The ranger path
- **Students build, not browse.** Every new diorama starts as the habitat's bare land, sky and climate — its creek, ponds and hills fitted to the chosen box size — and the student brings it to life with producers, plant-eaters, predators and decomposers.
- **An expedition is complete** when one of the student's own dioramas earns ⭐⭐ (10 quests). The quest tracker shows how many quests are left and what finishing will unlock.
- **Each completed expedition unlocks the next step:** start with the Backyard, Temperate Forest and Prairie, plus labels, landforms, painting, water, walking in, the food web and the guide; then
  1. 🥾 *Trail Scout*: Wetland & Savanna, **Code**, sculpt brushes, weather, mountain & island landforms, Large boxes;
  2. 🧭 *Explorer*: Desert, Taiga & the Blank box, guided tours, seasons, dune/mesa/canyon landforms;
  3. 🔭 *Field Naturalist*: Rainforest & Mountain Peaks, the Area tool, **Roam**, volcanoes, Huge and custom sizes;
  4. 🏅 *Habitat Expert*: Tundra & Rocky Shore, species from every habitat, habitat tiles;
  5. 🐋 *Ocean Explorer*: Coral Reef & Kelp Forest, AR/VR.
- A celebration lists everything a step unlocks. Locked tools and habitats show a padlock; tapping one explains what opens it. Quests for locked tools (code, tour) appear once they unlock.
- **Example dioramas** — the finished, fully stocked habitats — unlock per habitat once the student has completed an expedition there, to compare with their own. They don't count towards the path.
- **Teachers:** *Settings → Unlock everything (teacher mode)* on the home screen opens every habitat, tool and example on that device. Profiles from before the path count dioramas that already had ⭐⭐ as completed.

### 🏆 Play
- **Ranger profile:** pick an avatar and name; XP raises your level through ranks from *Seedling* to *Planet Keeper*. XP comes from discovering species, placing things and finishing quests.
- **Field Journal** (`J`): every species you tap in Explore is recorded with its card. Ones you haven't found yet show as silhouettes with a habitat hint, so there is always something left to find. Tap a discovered one for its full card: a real photo beside its 3D model, what it eats and what hunts it across its habitats, which habitats it lives in, how it is doing in the wild, a description, a range map and links to find out more. On phones the Journal is a tab of the studio: it pulls out over the diorama like the home screen's trays, keeping the top bar and tab bar, so another tab (or ✕, Back, or dragging it down) leaves it; a species card opens on top of it as a panel of its own with its own ✕ (the Journal's edge peeks out behind it), Back closes just the card and a tab leaves both.
- **Quests & stars** (`Q`, or tap the quest count under the diorama's name): each diorama has 16 quests (discover 5 species, plant 3 producers, build a 3-link food chain, add a decomposer, provide water, write labels and research notes, see it at night, walk inside, code something, give a tour…). 5, 10 and all 16 quests earn one, two and three stars. A tracker in the corner shows the next steps.
- **Ecosystem health** (0–100; tap the heart in the top bar: a popover on wide screens, a panel above the tab bar on phones and tablets) scores variety, producer/consumer/decomposer balance, whether every animal has food here (food it can really catch), the energy pyramid, food-chain length and water. It explains each part and suggests what to add next.
- **16 badges**, such as Food Chain Champ, Recycler, Night Owl, Storm Chaser, Four Seasons, Code Wrangler, Tour Guide, Ant's-Eye View and World Traveller, each celebrated with a banner, confetti and sound. Ecosystem badges come from the student's own changes: a goal that a ready-made habitat already meets counts only after it has been lost and reached again. Sounds and effects follow the Settings switches and *reduced motion*.

### 🧭 Explore
- Orbit, pinch-zoom and pan around a diorama that sits in a cardboard **shoebox** (or a **museum case** with a brass name plate, or a **floating island**). Walls fade away from any angle so nothing blocks the view.
- **Tap any organism** for an info card (the first tap adds it to the Field Journal): a **real photo** of the species with its 3D model in the corner, common & scientific name, Kingdom › Phylum › Class › Order › Family, producer/consumer/decomposer role, diet, IUCN status, a fact, real size and how much it is magnified, what it eats here and what eats it — plus the student's own research notes, where it stands on the IUCN Red List (a scale from Least Concern to Critically Endangered), a short description, a range map of where in the world it lives, and links to Wikipedia and iNaturalist (with how many times people have recorded it there). *Read* speaks it aloud.
- **Labels** puts paper flags on toothpicks next to every species (on by default, one flag per species; students can rewrite the text). When flags would overlap, the nearer ones, the selected object's and the students' own labels stay; the rest fade out until you zoom in.
- **Food web** shows who eats whom among the organisms actually in the diorama, laid out by trophic level, with sunlight under the producers and a recycling band where decomposers break down dead matter and return nutrients to the plants. Tap any organism to light up what it eats and what eats it, with its trophic level; **What if…?** shows who would go hungry if it disappeared (and which prey would lose a predator). Animals with nothing to eat are flagged, with species of the habitat that would feed them and an **Add** button. **Food chains** traces the longest chains to each top predator, and *Scavenging & eggs* adds those links on request.
- **How the web is worked out:** diets name foods (“small mammals”, “fish”, “nectar”) or exact species (pandas eat bamboo, monarch caterpillars milkweed). Every animal counts as the foods its class and body size make it, and hunters only take prey they can really catch — about their own size, far bigger for pack and ambush hunters (wolves take moose, lions zebra, crocodiles wildebeest), never top predators, and not prey too tiny to bother with (eagles don't hunt hummingbirds). Scavengers eat the remains of large animals, nest raiders eat birds' and reptiles' eggs, and parasites live on hosts of any size. Some food is always there without being placed: dead matter for decomposers, aphids on land plants, and plankton and algae wherever there is water. Partners (clownfish & anemone, cleaner shrimp & fish) never eat each other. The diagram, info cards, ecosystem health, quests, the tour order and the code blocks (*nearest food*, *predator within…*) all use this one model.
- **Guide** gives the habitat's climate, location, adaptations, threats, vocabulary and discussion questions, plus the live quest checklist — a ready-made rubric.
- **Walk in** drops you inside the diorama at the eye level of a person, fox, frog or ant (WASD, Shift to run), or lets you fly over it as a 🦅 bird (Space climbs, C dives). On touch, a joystick appears wherever your thumb lands; push it to the rim to run. ⬆ jumps; underwater, hold ⬆/⬇ to swim up and down.
- **Roam** walks you out of the box into an endless landscape of the same habitat. Around the box, the land is the diorama's own habitat carried on — the same hills and ground, its creek winding away across the country — joined seamlessly to its real edges, then it opens into a wider world of foothills, a few lakes and, further off, big mountain ranges. Only lakes and rivers hold water; other hollows are dry ground; its own plants grow across the world, its animals wander it, and grass and flowers carpet the ground near you. The top bar and labels step aside, a compass shows the way back (🏠 takes you there), and on browsers that allow it the view can go full screen. Nothing out there is saved: the land is the same each visit.
- **AR table** (phones/tablets with WebXR AR) places the diorama on a real table through the camera; **VR** (WebXR headsets) shows it at table height in front of you. These buttons only appear on devices that support them.
- **Time of day**, a day–night cycle, **weather** (clear, cloudy, rain, storm with lightning, snow, fog), **seasons** (trees and grass change colour; winter brings snow cover) and **wind**.
- Animals live on their own: they wander, graze, peck, circle overhead or swim in loops; fireflies and glowing fungi light up at night. Mammals walk, trot and gallop with their feet planted on the ground, changing gait with speed as real animals do; hoofed animals lie down to rest while dogs, cats and bears sit.

### 🔨 Build
- **Library** of nearly 350 things with 3D thumbnails, filtered to fit the current habitat (or *All*), searchable by name, scientific name, class or phylum, and colour-coded by food-web role, with marks for what is already in the diorama and what you have discovered. Tap to add near the centre of the view, or drag onto the diorama (drop an animal onto a plant or rock to perch it there).
- Drag objects to move them. A **context bar** above the selection (on phones, at the top of the Edit panel) turns, resizes, duplicates, focuses, deletes or opens it in Code; on touch, **long-press** picks an object up and a second finger **twists** and **pinches** it. Keys: `R` / `Shift+R` turn, `+` / `−` resize, `1` `2` `3` switch Explore / Build / Code.
- The inspector (Place · Label · Look · Info) sets direction, size, flying/swimming height, label text, research notes, animation, and every shape/colour parameter the asset module exposes (generated from its `params` schema).
- **Land tools** (World & land panel), in four tabs:
  - **Landforms** — stamp a hill, mountain (snow-capped when tall), volcano, mesa, plateau, ridge, cliff, canyon, valley, crater, lake, island or sand dune. Tap the land to place one, or press and drag to size it (stretched ones point the way you drag). A see-through preview shows the new shape first; size, height and direction sliders and *Paint the ground to match* (rocky cliffs, snowy peaks, sandy shores, layered canyon walls) are in the panel. `[` `]` resize, `R` turns.
  - **Area** — select some land with a **box** or **oval** (drag across it), the **lasso** (draw a loop around it) or the selection **brush** (paint over it; *Take away*, or Alt, paints land back out, and painting on any selection adds to it; `[` `]` size the brush). Pick what grows there — mountains, hills, plains, dunes, a canyon, mesas, volcanoes, a lake, a river, marsh, islands or a coast — and its ground style (suits the landform, this habitat, or any of 15 styles), height, ruggedness and how soft its edge is, then **Generate**. Mountains, hills, volcanoes, mesas, islands and dunes are made of the same landforms as the stamps, laid out to fill the shape (a range with one main summit, a chain of buttes, crescent dunes facing one wind), so an area looks just like stamped landforms; with *Suits the landform*, mountains, hills, volcanoes, mesas and canyons paint only themselves and leave the ground around them as it was. A canyon or a river follows the middle of the shape, so a winding band painted with the brush becomes a winding gorge or river. **Try another** swaps the result for a new variation; *Plants: some / lots* also plants it with species of that habitat; *Repaint the ground only* keeps the shape. A selection that reaches past the diorama's edge carries on through it, so a lake or river can meet the cut-away.
  - **Sculpt** — raise, dig, smooth, flatten and roughen brushes. They work like an airbrush: press and hold to keep building a hill or digging a hole in one spot, or drag to shape; only the area under the brush is rebuilt, so sculpting stays smooth on phones, and the grass rides along with the ground.
  - **Paint** — 22 ground types (grass, meadow, forest floor, dry grass, jungle, soil, mud, sand, desert sand, rock, gravel, tundra moss, snow, ice, scree, cliff rock, volcanic ash, red clay, salt flat, heather, marsh reeds, pebble beach) or **✨ Auto**, which picks the ground from the shape of the land; *Auto-paint the whole diorama* repaints everything to match.

  Neighbouring grounds blend into each other along ragged, natural edges (decided per pixel by the terrain shader), and grass, flowers and pebbles stop exactly where their ground does. New landforms and areas fade into the land around them. Lakes, rivers, islands and coasts add water when the diorama has none. Plus: water level and grass density. The front of the base is a cut-away showing soil horizons and rock strata, with a water cross-section where ponds meet the edge.
- **Top view** (`T`, or the map button over the 3D view) looks straight down with a metre grid — drag to pan, pinch or scroll to zoom — which makes selecting areas and placing landforms easy. Every land tool works in it.
- **Diorama size**: Small 24 × 16, Medium 40 × 28, Large 64 × 44 or Huge 96 × 64 m, or any size from 12 to 128 m. Growing adds new land of the same habitat around the edges — its hills, ground pattern and creek carry on, joined exactly to the old edge without stretching it — and shrinking trims them (anything left outside is removed, after asking). Every habitat picks its size when it is created; Large, Huge and custom sizes unlock along the ranger path.
- Swap the whole base for a habitat tile (coral reef, kelp forest, tide pools, beach, marsh, mangroves, frozen lake, lake, cave, groundwater cut-away).
- Undo/redo, duplicate, delete, autosave, download/open `.diorama.json` files, save a picture.

### 🧩 Code
A Blockly workspace with the Scratch-like *Zelos* renderer. Every object has its own scripts (like Scratch sprites) and there is a **World** stage for weather, time and camera. Press **Play** (green) to run everything; **Stop** puts every object back exactly as it was built. Click any stack to try just that stack. Running blocks glow. Each object offers one-tap **starter ideas** (wander, say hello when tapped, find food, run from predators, fly in circles, sleep at night, camouflage, quiz question, have a baby; for the World: day–night cycle, storm show, seasons, narrated intro). Biome templates come with a few scripts already — e.g. on the savanna the lioness hunts while zebras and gazelles flee.

| Category | Blocks |
|---|---|
| **Events** | when ▶ clicked · when this is tapped · when key pressed · every N seconds · when it becomes night/day · when I touch … · when I receive · broadcast (and wait) |
| **Motion** | move N m · turn ↻/↺ · walk/run to … · wander · follow · run away from · circle around · point in direction / towards · go to · go to x y · glide · fly to height · change height · jump · spin · if on edge, bounce · x, y, height, direction |
| **Looks** | say / think (for N s) · play animation (walk, trot, gallop, graze, flap, glide…) · animate automatically · turn joint (head, jaw, tail, wings…) · set/clear colour · glow · see-through · size · show/hide · set label · set any design parameter (e.g. coral *bleaching*) |
| **Sound** | make my animal sound · play sound (26 synthesised sounds) · speak text · volume |
| **Control** | wait · repeat · forever · if / if-else · wait until · repeat until · stop · clones |
| **Sensing** | touching? · distance to · in water? · nearest animal/plant/food/predator · *property* of … · ask & answer · key pressed? · is it night? · time of day · timer |
| **Ecosystem** | eat … · nearest food · nearest predator · predator within N m? · energy · grow · have a baby · sleep |
| **World** | time of day · day–night speed · weather · season · fog · wind · water level · captions · camera look/follow/overview · add a new species · shared values |
| **Operators / Variables / My Blocks** | maths, comparisons, logic, text, variables, and custom blocks (functions) |

Food relations come from a species database, so *nearest food* for a lion finds a zebra, and *nearest predator* for a zebra finds the lion.

Teachers will find ready-made **coding challenges** in *🎓 Learn → Teachers* (predator–prey chase, nocturnal owl, chameleon camouflage, water-cycle story, quiz, population model).

### 🎤 Present
**Tour** visits each species (the ones with research notes first) in food-chain order — producers, plant-eaters, predators, decomposers — flying the camera to each and optionally narrating the student's notes.

The plant library covers conifers (Norway spruce, balsam fir, Scots pine, Atlas cedar, larch, coast redwood, bald cypress), broadleaf and flowering trees (oak, birch, cherry, apple, hawthorn, baobab, kapok, mangrove), palms, desert plants, tundra shrubs, bamboo, banana and tree fern, plus wildflowers, grasses, ferns, mosses, lichens, aquatic plants and giant kelp. Trees follow the seasons (blossom, autumn colour, bare winter branches) and are built at three detail levels.

Small creatures are shown magnified so they can be seen (an ant-sized ladybird becomes ~35 cm; microscopic water bears are magnified ~600×). The info card says how much, and **Settings → True sizes** turns magnification off.

## Architecture

```
src/
  engine/        three.js world: Engine (renderer, camera, loop, picking, undo), Terrain (heightfield +
                 strata + paint), terrainGen (biome recipes as fields defined everywhere, fitted to a box size; carrying land on past the box for resizing and Roam), Water (depth-aware shader), Frame (shoebox /
                 museum / island + painted backdrop with a live sky shader), backdropPainter (procedural
                 landscape painting), Sky (time-of-day palette & dome), Groundcover (instanced grass,
                 flowers, pebbles, leaf litter), Weather, wind (shared sway patch), Actor (placed object:
                 animation clips, procedural rig idle/gait, ambient behaviour, labels, speech bubbles),
                 HeightProbe (GPU top-down height maps for habitat tiles), WalkMode (first person,
                 flying, roaming), XRMode (WebXR table-top AR/VR), ground (ground types, the per-pixel
                 ground shader with editor outlines, and its CPU twin), landforms (ground styles,
                 landform stamps, area generators built from the stamps), areaShape (selected areas:
                 boxes, ovals, and lasso or brush shapes as coverage plus a distance to the outline),
```

**How the reference assets are used.** Every module in `References/` exports `createAsset(params)`, a `params` schema and usually `rig`, `COLORWAYS` and `userData.update/setNight`. `src/assets/modules.ts` registers them with `import.meta.glob`, so each becomes its own chunk. `builder.ts` adds per-quality parameters (e.g. draft rigs and lighter plant foliage on phones), turns Wildform animals to face +Z, consolidates their meshes per animated joint, caches a prototype per parameter set and clones it (skinned rigs via `SkeletonUtils`), and discovers animation clips (mammal & bird rigs), rig pivots (reptiles, Wildform) and update hooks (water-world, aquarium). The inspector UI is generated from each module's own `params` schema.

**Code execution.** Each object's workspace compiles to plain async JavaScript: hats become entries in a factory, loops yield every frame (`await api.tick()`), and every block calls a thread-local API. Calling the factory creates fresh closures, which gives each object — and each clone — its own variables. Threads are cancelled cooperatively on Stop and the scene snapshot is restored.

### Adding a species or a biome
- **Species:** add a row to the matching `src/assets/data-*.ts` table (or an `add({...})` entry in `catalog.ts`) with its asset module, params, biomes, taxonomy, diet, food tags (`tags` it *is*, `eats` it eats) and a fact. A new tree or herb is a species entry in `src/assets/flora/trees.ts` or `herbs.ts` plus an `FL(...)` / `HB(...)` line in `catalog.ts`.
- **Biome:** add a template to `src/biomes/templates.ts` with a terrain recipe or a habitat tile, environment, placements (fixed `at` or scattered `count` + `area` with `where: 'dry' | 'wet'`) and a guide. Attach starter scripts with `scriptsFor(['wander', 'hello'], name)`.

### Mammal legs and gaits
The rigged mammals (`References/Mammals/src/assets/animals/mammal-rig.mjs`, revisions 4–10) and birds (`References/Birds/src/assets/bird-rig.mjs`, revisions 8 and 10) were checked against skeletons and the species photos:

- **Hind legs from real proportions.** Each joint is placed as a fraction of the hip-joint height (`HIND_LAYOUT`, tuned per species with `hind: {...}`): the stifle high in the flank fold and well forward of the hip, the hock about a third of the way up under the point of buttock, the fetlock at 12–19 % with a sloping pastern (it was at 4–7 %, so hooves sat on the cannon). Plantigrade shins (bears, raccoons, badgers, anteaters) stand upright over a heel below the hip instead of slanting back; rabbits', squirrels' and mice's folded legs stay inside a wedge-shaped haunch with the long foot flat, instead of hanging below the body.
- **Muscle, not pipes.** The hind leg is lofted through its side-view outline — the thigh's front edge from the flank fold to the stifle, its back edge (the breeches) from the point of buttock to the gaskin, then the Achilles tendon to the point of hock — and skinned by joint planes. Hooves have a coronary band that drops to the heels and a sloped toe wall (two claws for cloven feet, with dewclaws); paws are a round pad with arched toes.
- **Planted-foot gaits.** Every foot follows a path — planted and carried back at body speed in stance, lifted and swung forward in swing, the hoof breaking over at push-off — and each leg is solved to reach it (hind legs as a pantograph; forelegs with the shoulder blade gliding with the forearm and the knee locked in stance). The body sinks where the legs need it at double support. Footfalls follow each family: a lateral-sequence walk; a diagonal trot, or a pace for camels, llamas and giraffes; a transverse gallop (horses, cattle, rhinos) or rotary gallop with spine flexion (cats, dogs, deer); a half-bound for rabbits, squirrels and otters; an amble for elephants, which never gallop. Clips are timed by the Froude number from leg length, short-legged animals take shorter, quicker steps, and every gait clip records its stride.
- **Bodies, not barrels** (revision 5). Every torso section used to be the same oval and the legs were tubes plugged in underneath, so bodies read as barrels on sticks. Now each station has its own section shape — narrow withers and a keeled chest, a broad flat back and croup, a belly that hangs in grazers (cattle, zebras, donkeys) and tucks up in dogs, cats and deer — and the breastbone rises to the base of the neck instead of a flat box front. Seen from above, the chest is narrower than the ribs, with a waist and broad quarters. The shoulder blade, triceps and point of shoulder are sculpted into the chest wall; the forelegs are lofted through their side-view outline (shoulder, upper arm, a muscular forearm tapering to the knee), with heavier limbs for big cats and bears; cats get their arched loin. The thigh's root now stays inside the hip (it showed as knobs on the croup), and normals are blended across the body–leg seams so each join shades as one surface. Body lengths were checked against real point-of-shoulder to point-of-buttock measurements and were already within about 10 %.
- **Heads** (revision 6). The lower jaw used to hang below the whole head as a narrow strap, like a crocodile's mouth; now the cheek covers the jaw behind the corner of the mouth (under the eye in hunters, far forward in grazers and rodents) and the lips meet only in front of it, while the mouth still opens to roar. Every head has a real stop (the forehead rising over the eyes), cats have deep, round skulls with blunt muzzles, and the Labrador a broad skull, deep muzzle and ears hanging flat against its cheeks. The knobs at the base of every ear are gone. The elephant's head was a flat helmet aimed straight down with its ears buried in the neck; it now has a domed head and great fan-shaped ears draped behind the eyes over the shoulders, like an African elephant's. Hippos have a vast boxy muzzle with raised eyes, and camels a long slender face.
- **Humps, heads, limb roots and feet** (revision 7). The bison's front was a tall tent and the boar's withers a sharp peak: a big withers bump now falls steeply in front, slopes gradually back to the loin and keeps the body's width up to a rounded top, and the bison has a shaggy cape whose ragged edge runs back over the hump and down the forearms. Wolves, bears, otters and rodents had no nose (it was buried in the rounded muzzle); big ears that crossed over the crown (coyote, fennec) now stand apart; a Labrador's hanging ears no longer kink. The camel has a long, slender head with a thick split upper lip hanging over the lower and slit nostrils, the moose cupped palms tilted up and back with tines round their edges, the sheep a Roman nose and a wool cap, and the otter, beaver and polar bear their real head shapes. Where the legs leave the body, the thigh's top no longer stands out of the croup as a slab and each leg flares into the body instead of meeting it at a hard crease. The elephant has high shoulders over a sloping back, a deep round belly and fan ears with a curled rim and soft folds, and its pad feet (and the rhino's and hippo's) stay on the ground instead of rolling 2–5 cm into it.
- **Planted feet, without hitches** (revision 7). The body used to jump up by up to a quarter of a metre the moment a straining foot lifted; that drop is now smoothed through the cycle. A rolling pelvis and swaying body no longer slide planted feet sideways (5–15 cm a step in walks): each leg abducts to keep its foot on its line, and the foot stays level. Feet lift and set down nearly vertically instead of skidding, and clips carry more keys, so fast feet no longer dip into the ground between them. Across all 126 gait clips, sideways slip fell from 2.2 to 0.1 cm and slip along the walk from 5.2 to 1.4 cm, and no foot goes deeper than before.
- **The African elephant** (revision 8) has its own builder (`elephantBuild`), sharing the rig's lofts, skinning, coat painter and leg solver. A bull is highest at the withers, the back dipping and rising again to the hips, with a deep belly and pillar legs whose elbow and stifle sit low (the stifle bulging forward at the flank) over round fore and oval hind pads with flat pale toenails, four in front and three behind. The large head has a single dome, a small low eye with the temporal hollow above and behind it and a pointed lower lip; the trunk, one loft with the head, is wide at its base, tapers to two fingers a hand above the ground and is ringed with creases, on five bones (`Trunk1`–`Trunk5`). The tusks grow from skin sheaths and curve down, forward and up. The Africa-shaped ears hang from above and behind the eye to the corner of the mouth and lie back over the neck and shoulder as a curved shell with a rolled top edge and a thick rim; they flap on `EarL`/`EarR` and never cut into the body. The grey-brown skin is wrinkled: creases painted from a tiling cell pattern, and real rings on the trunk and lower legs. The walk is a lateral walk on straight legs whose knees bend back in the swing, with body sway, a nodding head, a swinging trunk and fanning ears; the amble (its `trot` clip) keeps a foot on the ground; idle shifts its weight and swings and curls the trunk; graze plucks grass with the trunk tip and brings it to the mouth; alert spreads the ears and raises the head; roar trumpets with the trunk raised; sit lies the elephant down on its side, clear of the ground. In both gaits every foot stays within half a millimetre of the ground and slips under a centimetre a step.
- **Gait keys where joints turn fast** (revision 8). A clip's keys are now added where a joint turns more than 1.5° between two keys, so fast feet no longer dip between them (the giraffe's and llama's gallops sank 1.5 cm; across all 126 gait clips the deepest foot is now 1 cm down, the polar bear's gallop, and none goes deeper than before) while slow clips stay light.
- **Birds walk on planted feet** (revision 8). The bird walk swung each leg on a sine while the app played it at a fixed rate, so walking birds slid about 1.8 hip heights a step and every bird stood with its toes 2–30 mm in the ground. Each toe tip now stays put through the stance (the heel rolling up onto it at the end); the foot is then picked up, carried forward with its toes hanging and set down flat. The hip, knee and intertarsal joint reach it by inverse kinematics; the pelvis rolls and yaws by family (waterfowl and penguins waddle, rocking over the stance foot); the head keeps its attitude, and pigeons and fowl hold it still between thrusts. Stride, crouch and timing follow each bird's leg (`WALK_STYLE`); the hips and tail lift wherever the body would touch the ground. Toes, talons and the end of the tarsus rest on the ground, and penguins, ostriches and kiwis flap their wings standing instead of lying face down in a flight posture.
- **Faces, heads and bodies** (revision 9). Every mammal was checked against its photo from the side, the front and close up. The lower jaw was a narrow strap hung below a rounded muzzle (a duck's bill on cats, a crocodile's mouth on wolves): it now fits up inside the upper lips, as wide as the muzzle, with a rounded chin, a dark lip line on carnivores and the canines tucked inside the lips. Heads were tapered tubes; each part of the head now has its own width above and below (crown, forehead, nose bridge, cheeks, lips), so cats have round, broad-cheeked faces with forward eyes, dogs and wolves a domed forehead over a real stop, a narrow nose bridge and broad lips, hoofed animals a broad flat forehead over a narrower face, round jowls and a narrow throatlatch, and bears a big head with a dished face. Short necks no longer end in a collar behind the skull. Species fixes include the horse, cow (broad poll and square muzzle), pig (fat jowls, sturdier legs), Labrador (flat-topped skull, long deep muzzle), hyena, brown bear, panda, badger, the big cats, bison (a massive head and short beard; the goat's and bison's beards were never built), boar (deep body), hippo (a head a third longer), camel and giraffe (lean quarters under a falling croup), and the remapped snow leopard, arctic and fennec foxes. Horns: the rhino's sweeps back with a concave taper, the ram's curl wraps the ear instead of jutting past the jaw, a pronghorn has its forward prong and a mountain goat slender dagger horns. The marine mammals were rebuilt from real proportions: the dolphin (it was a football 0.78 m deep; now slender, with a melon dropping to a short beak, a falcate fin and swept, notched flukes), the humpback (stocky, with a broad flat rostrum, a pleated throat and long white flippers held out), the harp seal (a spindle a quarter as wide as it is long with a raised head and harp arms sweeping down the flanks; it was an egg twice as wide) and the surfacing whale (flipper blades instead of sticks, and flukes that no longer read as a saw). Ground contact through every gait is unchanged (within 0.03 cm), and the library thumbnails are re-rendered (cache v10).
- **Measured layouts** (revision 10). However its proportions were tuned, the parametric layout made tubes on sticks: thin necks on horses and wolves, pencil forearms under big cats, a giraffe's body a pinched wedge on spindles, a bear with a dog's head and no hump, a tiger like a weasel. Every mammal is now laid out the way the elephant was, from a measured reference animal (`REF` in the rig): the side and top outline of its body and neck at stations, its joints, and each leg's front and back edges and width down to the hoof or paw, in fractions of its shoulder height, read from conformation measurements, skeletons and photographs — a 1.60 m riding horse for the equids (body as long as the horse is tall, the chest 48 % of its height, elbow on the girth line, knee at 30 %, hock at 38 %, fetlock at 16 %), a Holstein, a bison bull, a white-tailed deer, a moose, a bighorn, a dromedary, a llama, a giraffe, a pig and a boar, a white rhino, a hippo, a lion, a cheetah, a wolf, a spotted hyena, a brown bear, a polar bear, a raccoon, a badger, an otter, a rabbit, a squirrel, a capybara, a beaver and a giant anteater. Related species tune a table (`refLen`, `refDepth`, `refW`, `refNeck`, `foreBulkR`, `hindBulkR`): a stockier zebra, a slim gazelle, a deep reindeer, a woolly sheep, a broad Labrador, a long fox. Hoofed, paw and sole legs share outlines measured on a horse, a lion and a bear, so a horse has a deep, crested neck, a full barrel, muscular forearms and gaskins over thin cannons; a bear its shoulder hump and big head; a giraffe a deep chest sloping to its croup; a camel its U-shaped neck and lean, free thigh; a rabbit its egg-shaped hunch over the haunches. The layout feeds the shared machinery, so heads, ears, horns, manes, tails, feet, the coat painter, bones and clips work as before (the elephant keeps its own; the mouse and hedgehog followed in revision 11). Also: the lion's mane frames its face, the panda's black saddle lies over its shoulders, the Bactrian camel's humps are rounded.
- **Lying and sitting on the ground** (revision 10). Lying down (hoofed animals) and sitting (carnivores, rodents) lower the body only as far as the folded legs' own surfaces, hooves, pads and paws stay on top of the ground, as the elephant's lying down did: hoofed animals lying down sank their knees, hocks and cannons 10 cm into it on average (the giraffe 23 cm); now under 1.3 cm. Tails lie along the ground instead of through it (a sitting lion's tail went 65 cm under, a wolf's 23; a lying horse's tail hair 60). Across all 126 gait clips the deepest foot is now 0.9 cm down (it was 1.6) and slip is unchanged (`node tools/gait-check.mjs`).
- **Birds, turtles and crocodiles** (revision 10). Raptors, parrots, the cockatoo, hornbill and toucan perch upright instead of hunching like vultures, the eagle and falcon with their heads over the chest; the eagle's, falcon's, macaw's and cockatoo's bills are a third smaller; an owl's eyes look forward out of its facial disc (they looked sideways from the middle of the skull, like a frog's); the albatross has a short thick neck, the pelican an S-curved one over a bigger body and the ostrich a big plumed body. Tortoise and sea-turtle shells have real scutes (hexagonal vertebrals, costals set between them, marginals round the rim, grooved seams and growth rings) instead of a checkerboard of rectangles, and the Nile crocodile a body as deep and broad as a real one's.
- **In the app** (`src/engine/Actor.ts`) gaits change by Froude number (walk below ~0.5, trot to ~2.5, then gallop, with hysteresis) and play at the rate that carries the planted feet back at ground speed, so they no longer slide. The rate follows a quick estimate of the speed and can go down to a tenth of the clip's own, so a slow walk keeps its feet planted too (a zebra's feet slid 8 cm a step at 1 m/s and 14 cm at 0.3 m/s; now about 1.5), animals moving slowly walk slowly instead of gliding, and ambient walkers ease into their pace. Graze swings the neck down about its base until the muzzle meets the grass in front of the forefeet (short-necked grazers step their forefeet forward rather than folding their elbows); carnivores, bears and rodents sniff the ground in front of their forefeet instead. Alert lifts the head (deer and antelope raise a forefoot), and *sit* is a sit for carnivores, a sit-up with raised forepaws for rabbits and squirrels, and lying down for hoofed animals and elephants. Since revision 8 a bird's walk carries its stride too and plays the same way (a bird on the ground walks at most 1.3 times its walk's own pace; flying birds used to cross the ground at their flying speed), a gait fading out into a standing clip holds its pose instead of walking its planted feet back under a stopped animal, and the pivot legs of lizards, tortoises and salamanders step at the cadence whose back-swing keeps pace with the ground (it was fixed whatever the legs' length, so small lizards' feet skidded about 15 cm a step).

- **Reptiles and amphibians on one rig** (revision 11). Every reptile and amphibian — the 20 catalog species and the sand lizard, green anole, American alligator, box turtle, garter snake, alpine newt, common toad and glass frog that were Wildform plans — is built by `References/Reptiles/src/herp-rig.mjs`, which replaces the per-species modules (kept for the Reptiles reference app). Each animal is laid out from a measured reference in fractions of its snout–vent, total or carapace length, on one of four body plans: *saurian* (lizards, the tuatara, chameleons, crocodilians, salamanders: one surface from inside the skull to the tail tip, sprawling legs solved from each girdle joint to where the foot rests; crests, dewlaps, the frilled lizard's frill, the chameleon's casque and turret eyes, osteoderms, teeth, gills, fins, skin folds), *ophidian* (snakes and the caecilian on 30–45 bones, resting in an S; the cobra's hood, the sidewinder's horns and rattle), *chelonian* (a thick shell with painted vertebral, costal and marginal scutes and growth rings, plastron and bridges; legs or flippers) and *anuran* (frogs and toads sitting nose-up, the hind legs folded in a Z, a pumping throat). The skin is painted with scales (granular, keeled or plates; snakes' wide belly plates) and markings into one texture. Clips plant the feet: lizards walk and trot with the girdles turning in a standing wave, crocodilians and tortoises walk in lateral sequence, the frilled lizard runs on its hind legs, snakes undulate along one track or sidewind like a rolling helix, frogs hop, toads walk; displays spread the frill, fan the dewlap, rear the cobra with its hood, shoot the chameleon's tongue and swell a frog's throat; tortoises withdraw into their shells; swimmers (crocodilians, salamanders, the sea turtle) swim when the actor is in the water. `node tools/herp-gait-check.mjs` measures ground contact and slip for every clip (feet within a millimetre or two in the gaits).
- **Birds from measurements** (revision 11). Every bird's anatomy is derived from field-guide measurements of an adult (total length, wingspan, wing chord, tail, tarsus, culmen, bill depth, head width and carried neck; `MEASURED` in the bird rig): heads had been 1.5–2 times too large, tails and legs guessed. Closed tails are a quarter as wide as long and follow the line of the back; wingspans are the real ones (an albatross 3.1 m, an eagle 2 m); a peacock's train is 1.5 m.
- **Mouse and hedgehog** (revision 11) are laid out from measured references too: the mouse crouched on high, round haunches with big ears, the hedgehog a dome of spines on short legs (it stood like a small bear).

### Animal workbench
A live view and code editor for every animal in the library, for checking and improving their accuracy:

```bash
npm run workbench
```

It opens `workbench.html` on the dev server (dev only; it is not part of the app build). It saves files only for requests from this machine: another device on the network can view it but not save.

- **Pick an animal** from the library on the left (search by name, scientific name or family; ↑ ↓ step through). It is built through the app's own builder, so what you see is what the app makes: orbit it, jump to the camera views (`1`–`7`, `8`/`9` for a close-up of the face), play and scrub its clips, see the wireframe, skeleton and bounds, change the lighting and the graphics tier, and switch between its true size and the magnified size small animals get in the app. A grid and a red marker (the way it should face) give scale and direction.
- **Compare it with the real animal:** its photo, taxonomy, real size and description sit beside the view, and *Overlay on 3D* lays the photo over the model (drag to move, wheel to scale, flip) to match silhouettes. *Snapshot* keeps captures to compare against later; *Sheet* makes one image of the photo beside the model from several views.
- **Params** shows the exact parameters the app builds it with, as editable code (for rigged mammals and birds, `specOverride` reaches every anatomical and colour setting of the species preset), plus controls generated from the module's own schema. Every change rebuilds the model live; edits are kept per animal until you *Save to tuning.json*, which writes the differences to `src/assets/tuning.json` for the app.
- **Code** opens the source that defines it — the asset module, its species preset, the family defaults it inherits, the painter that colours it, its catalog rows and any remap onto another rig — with buttons that jump to each part (for mammals also the species' measured reference table and layout, the shared leg profiles, ground clearance, the limb-root blend, foot paths, leg solver, line keeping, girdle-drop smoothing and clip baking; for the elephant its own builder, skin creases and clips; for birds the walk and its set-up). `Ctrl`/`Cmd`+`S` writes the file; an asset module is re-imported in place and the model rebuilds in under a second, without reloading the page. *Revert to HEAD* restores the committed text.
- **Spec** lists what the build produced (size, triangles, clips, rig pivots, colour zones, the resolved anatomy) and **Diff** shows `git diff` of this animal's files.
- **From scripts:** the page exposes `window.wb` (select, setParams/patchParams, view, clip, capture, sheet, code.open/setText/save…), and `node tools/workbench.mjs` drives it with Playwright: `sheet --ids mammal/zebra,bird/owl --views front34,side,head --out shots/wb` saves photo-and-model sheets (`--group Mammals` for a whole library group, `--params '{"specOverride":{…}}'` to try a change without saving it), and `ui --id mammal/lion --tab code --out shots/wb.png` captures the whole workbench. It starts its own dev server, or uses one given with `--url`.

### Developer tools
Run these with `npm run dev` stopped (the review and UI scripts start their own Vite server); they use the Playwright Chromium already on the machine.

- **Animal workbench** — see [above](#animal-workbench); `node tools/workbench.mjs` renders it from scripts.

- **Asset review harness** — `review.html` renders catalog assets through the app's own builder from several views (front ¾, side, top, with a marker showing which way is forward). Query parameters pick what to show: `ids=`, `cat=`, `group=`, `family=`, `match=`, `views=`, `clip=`, `season=`, `q=` (quality), or variants of one asset with `base=<id>&v=[{…},…]`. `node tools/review-assets.mjs --query "cat=animals&cols=3" --per 15 --out shots/animals` saves contact sheets.
- **App icon** — `node tools/make-icons.mjs` draws the icon (a shoebox diorama: an open cardboard box with a tree, a fox and a painted sky inside) and writes `public/favicon.svg`, `public/icons/icon.svg` and the PNG icons for the manifest and iOS, including a maskable one whose art stays inside Android's safe zone. `--out dir` writes a preview set elsewhere.
- **Screenshots** — `node tools/shoot.mjs` captures app URLs (desktop or `--device phone`) and prints triangles, draw calls and actor counts.
- **UI smoke test** — `node tools/ui-check.mjs --out shots/ui-check` taps an animal (field card + discovery), selects it in Build (context bar, resize), twists and pinches it with two fingers, resizes the phone panel by its header and taps a panel tab, on desktop and phone, and reports any page errors.
- **Herp gait check** — `node tools/herp-gait-check.mjs` does the same for every reptile and amphibian clip (snakes, turtles and frogs included).
- **Gait and ground-contact check** — `node tools/gait-check.mjs` builds every rigged mammal, plays each clip at 160 phases (between the keys too) and reports how far any foot sinks below the ground, how far planted feet slip along and across the walk, and how far the tail goes under (cm). `--only horse,lion` narrows it, `--save base.json` keeps the numbers and `--compare base.json` lists what got worse since (exiting non-zero if anything did).
- **Food-web check** — `node tools/foodweb-check.mjs` runs the who-eats-whom cases in `tools/foodweb-cases.js` (size limits, specialists, scavenging, eggs, parasites, partners) and lists every habitat's example diorama with its longest food chain and any animal with nothing to eat. It exits non-zero if a case fails.
- **Species photos, maps and descriptions** — `node tools/fetch-species-media.mjs` (run it with `NODE_USE_ENV_PROXY=1` behind a proxy) finds each living species on Wikidata and takes its own picture and range map from Wikimedia Commons, but only files whose licence lets anyone reuse them (public domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA, read from the file page's machine-readable licence data; GFDL-only and non-free files are skipped). Where Commons has no such photo it uses a curated or the most-favourited research-grade iNaturalist photo under one of those licences. The description is the opening of the species' Wikipedia article (CC BY-SA, via iNaturalist), shortened. Pictures are resized to WebP in `public/species/`, with credits, descriptions, the IUCN category and links in `public/species/media.json` and a credits table in `public/species/CREDITS.md`. Responses are cached in `.cache/species-media`; it only fetches species that are new (`--only id,id` redoes some, `--all` redoes every one from the cache, `--refresh` fetches again). `tools/species-media-pins.json` can pin a better picture for a species (`{"mammal/cat": {"commons": "File name.jpg"}}` or `{"inat": photoId}`).
- **Home & Back-button check** — `node tools/home-check.mjs --out shots/home-check` opens each tray from the phone tab bar, steps back out of a habitat briefing, starts an expedition, opens and closes panels in the studio (More, the quests, the heart's panel, the food web, the guide, a locked tool's explanation, the journal and a species card on top of it) and the home screen's Settings, reloads, drags trays and panels closed, and checks that every panel sits between the top bar and the tab bar with both still usable and its own ✕ reachable (even while a celebration banner shows), that one panel replaces another, that any tab leaves them, and that Back always closes the top-most layer without leaving dead history entries. It exits non-zero if a check fails.
- **Ranger training check** — `node tools/learn-check.mjs --out shots/learn-check` starts a fresh device's first expedition, takes the guided start and follows the first unit by really doing each step (dragging, pinching and sliding the camera, adding a plant and a plant-eater from the inventory, moving, turning and resizing one, undoing, tapping a species, opening the journal), then checks the Learn hub, a quest's *Show me how* and the home screen's progress, on a phone and a desktop. `--suite tips` declines the guided start and checks the one-time tips; `--suite more` (teacher mode, the Forest example) starts every other lesson from the hub and completes it — stamping, painting and sculpting the land, writing labels, walking with the keyboard or the joystick, running code, touring and roaming. `--only phone|desktop` and `--lessons a,b` narrow it down. It exits non-zero if a check fails.

## Devices & performance

- Built on standard WebGL 2, WebAudio and Speech Synthesis, for current desktop and mobile browsers. Automated checks run in Chromium at desktop and phone sizes.
- Phones: in Build and Code the panel takes most of the screen under a smaller 3D view that always stays visible; drag the panel's header to give either more room (it snaps, and flings). Held sideways, the panel sits beside the view and the mode tabs move into the top bar. While the camera is at its overview it re-fits the diorama whenever the view changes shape. Tapping an organism in Explore glides the camera so it sits in the space left by its species card.
- App feel: every screen has the same top bar and, on phones, the same bottom tab bar. On phones and tablets nothing covers them: the food web, guide, quests, Learn & help, Settings, More, the heart's details, the Journal, species cards and "Not unlocked yet" all open as **panels** that pull out over the diorama (or the home screen) between the top bar and the tab bar. Both bars keep working — any tab leaves a panel, and opening another panel replaces it — and every panel keeps its title bar with ✕ on screen, because it is sized by the room between the bars rather than by viewport units (which phone browsers measure past their toolbars). Celebration banners, XP and messages pop up over the top bar there, and lesson cards stay clear of a panel's title bar, so nothing sits on a ✕. On wide screens these are dialogs (the heart a popover, More a menu). The phone's Back button (or the browser's) closes whatever is on top — a panel, a tray, a habitat briefing — and leaves a diorama for the home screen, instead of leaving the site. Panels and trays can be dragged down by their title bar to close, or closed by tapping the dimmed diorama above them. *Add to Home Screen* (or *Install app*) opens it full screen with its own icon; the bars then extend under the status bar and home indicator.
- Touch: a bottom tab bar, undo/redo in the Build panel, one-finger orbit / two-finger pan-zoom, long-press to pick up, two-finger twist and pinch on a held object, forgiving taps on thin things like legs and branches, short haptic ticks where the device supports them, and safe-area padding for notched phones.
- Three graphics tiers (Fast / Balanced / Detailed) pick model detail, shadow resolution and grass density; the renderer also adapts its resolution to keep the frame rate up. Phones default to Fast. In Roam they also set how far you can see (about 145, 175 or 210 m) and how many plants and animals fill the world; its land is built a few milliseconds per frame, nearest first, coarser further away.
- Land grids are 25 cm for most dioramas and a little coarser for huge ones, so every size stays smooth to sculpt; huge dioramas keep a shorter undo history.
- All geometry is procedural and needs no image files (the ground shader's speckle comes from a small noise texture generated at start-up, and the rigged mammals and birds paint their coat patterns into a small texture as they are built); the only network requests are fonts (optional — system fonts are the fallback). The species photos and range maps ship with the app (about 300 small WebP pictures in `public/species/`, loaded only when a card opens), so they work offline too; their links to Wikipedia, iNaturalist, Commons and the IUCN Red List open in a new tab. Library thumbnails are rendered in the background on first view and cached in IndexedDB.
